Boundaries in relationships

  • Personal boundaries
  • Conflict
  • Communication
  • Making requests

A boundary is not a wall but the place where I end and the other person begins. It defines what is acceptable to me and what isn’t, and it is communicated in words rather than expected.

Typical difficulties: boundaries are not recognized (the person realizes something is wrong only once anger has already built up), not voiced (the other person “should guess”), voiced without consequences (the person says it and then keeps putting up with it).

Boundaries can be too rigid, and then closeness becomes impossible, or too blurred, when a person dissolves in other people’s needs. Both patterns are often learned in childhood.

The boundary formula has three parts: what exactly is unacceptable, what I ask for instead, and what I will do if it continues. Without the third part, a boundary remains a wish.

A common fear of clients: “if I say it, the relationship will fall apart.” Sometimes relationships really do change, and this is worth discussing in advance. If there is a risk of violence, plan for safety first, not for a direct conversation.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The topic of boundaries was systematized in the work of Henry Cloud and John Townsend, as well as in assertiveness training.
