Exercise

My strengths

  • Recovery
  • Authoring your life
  • Self-criticism
  • Self-worth
  • Self-confidence
  • Supportive self-talk

Goal: name one’s own strengths and back each with a real episode, so that the self-description rests on experience rather than general words.

Approximate time: 25-40 minutes.

Materials: a sheet divided into two columns: “Strength” and “When it showed.”

  1. 1.Name the strengths

Ask the client to name 5-8 traits or skills they can consider their strengths. If this is hard, offer prompts: persistence, kindness, honesty, humour, curiosity, listening, courage, being organised, fairness.

  1. 2.Find an episode

For each trait the client recalls a specific situation: when, where, what they did. “Without an example the trait doesn’t count”: this isn’t a test, but a way to make it tangible. If no example comes, the trait can stay “on hold.”

  1. 3.Look at the examples
  • ✓Which of these episodes do you most enjoy remembering?
  • ✓What in it did you yourself do?
  • ✓Who among the people close to you would notice this trait in you?
  1. 4.Find a use
  • ✓Which strength could help you in your current situation or request?
  • ✓How could you use it this week?
  1. 5.Close

Ask the client to choose one trait and plan a small step where it will be needed. At the next meeting, discuss how it went.

With teenagers aged 15-18

You can start with what friends, a coach or teachers say about the teenager and then check: “Do you agree? When did that happen?” Examples from sport, school, friendship and online communities count just as much.

Note for the practitioner

People with strong self-criticism may discount every example. Don’t argue; ask: “If your friend had done this, what would you call it?”

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

Based on the idea of character strengths from positive psychology (Christopher Peterson, Martin Seligman, “Character Strengths and Virtues,” 2004). The requirement to back each trait with an episode and the steps are a Metafora editorial instruction.
