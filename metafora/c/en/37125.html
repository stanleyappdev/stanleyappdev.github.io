Exercise

An orange for two

  • Negotiation
  • Conflict
  • Making requests
  • Communication

Two sisters can’t agree on how to share the last orange and end up cutting it in half. One eats the pulp, the other grates the peel into her dough, and both throw away the rest. Each could have gotten twice as much if someone had asked: “What do you need the orange for?”

Roger Fisher and William Ury advised distinguishing a position (what a person demands) from an interest (why they want it). People argue over positions, but interests can often be reconciled.

How to run

  1. 1.Write down both sides’ positions: “I want to come home at 11 p.m.” - “No later than 9 p.m.”
  1. 2.Ask yourself two or three times why you want it: “To be with my friends until the end of the evening” → “So I’m not always the one who leaves first.”
  1. 3.Try to guess the other side’s interests: “To know that my child is safe.” Check your guesses in conversation.
  1. 4.Come up with at least three options that take both sides’ interests into account:
  • ▫11 p.m., but with a call at 9 p.m. and coming home together with friends
  • ▫later on Fridays, earlier on weekdays.
  1. 5.Find an objective criterion both can rely on: for example, the public transport schedule.
  1. 6.Think through a backup plan in case you can’t reach agreement: having one makes negotiating calmer.

Question: what changes in the conversation when you ask “Why do you need this?” instead of “Why are you being so stubborn?”

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

Principled negotiation: Roger Fisher and William Ury, Getting to Yes (1981).
