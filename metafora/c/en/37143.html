Puddles of children’s grief

  • Loss
  • Children's emotions
  • Support
  • Sadness

A handout for adults who are troubled that a child is grieving “the wrong way” after a loss: crying one moment and asking for ice cream and running off to play the next. This is not indifference. Children often grieve in bursts: they jump into a puddle of sadness and jump out again, because they can’t yet bear a strong feeling for long. Adult grief is more like a river that takes a long time to wade across.

What helps

- Tell the truth in simple words, without “fell asleep” or “went away”: such explanations frighten the child or make them wait.

- Be there when the child “jumps into the puddle,” and don’t pull them into it when they’re playing.

- Answer the same questions as many times as the child asks them.

- Keep the usual routine: sleep, daycare or school, bedtime rituals; tell daycare staff and teachers about the loss.

- Don’t hide your own sadness: it helps a child to see that an adult can cry and still be there for them.

When to see a professional: the child stops playing and enjoying things for a long time, returns for a long time to behavior they had outgrown, or is constantly afraid for the lives of loved ones. If the child often says they want to be with the person who died or that they don’t want to live, contact a child psychologist or doctor the same day.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The image of “puddles of grief” to explain how children grieve is used by Winston’s Wish, a UK charity that supports bereaved children.
