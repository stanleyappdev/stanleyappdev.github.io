The exaggeration experiment

  • Awareness of emotions
  • Body awareness
  • Expressing emotions
  • Recognizing and naming emotions

A habitual gesture, posture or intonation often expresses a feeling the person doesn’t notice. When it is amplified, the feeling becomes noticeable.

How to run

  1. 1.While the client is talking, notice a small repeated movement or intonation: a palm rubbing a knee, shoulders creeping up, a smile while describing pain, a voice that fades at the end of sentences.
  1. 2.Name it neutrally and ask permission: “I notice you smile every time you talk about your brother. Would you like to explore that?”
  1. 3.“Do it a little more strongly… a bit more… and now more slowly.” If it’s an intonation, ask the client to say the same phrase louder or quieter.
  1. 4.“What do you feel? If this movement could speak, what would it say?” For example:
  • ▫“I’m pretending everything is fine”
  • ▫“I don’t want to talk about this.”
  1. 5.If the client wants, try the opposite (relaxing the face) and compare.
  1. 6.Go back to the topic: what has become clearer?

What to watch for: don’t interpret (“that’s anger”). The client finds the meaning; if nothing comes up, don’t push.

In a group, one volunteer works while the others only observe; or in pairs: the partner notices a gesture and the speaker amplifies it.

Caution: don’t mimic or mock. Don’t amplify movements that cause pain or strain an injured area. If a strong feeling or an old memory comes up, slow down and return to the breath and a sense of support.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The “Exaggeration” game is described in Abraham Levitsky and Fritz Perls’s article “The Rules and Games of Gestalt Therapy” (1970).
