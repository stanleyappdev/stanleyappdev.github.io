Fight, flight and freeze responses

  • Fear
  • Safety
  • PTSD
  • Shame and guilt
  • Recognizing and naming emotions

Faced with a threat, the body chooses one of these responses without conscious involvement. Fight-anger, tension, readiness to attack. Flight-anxiety, restlessness, an urge to get out of the situation. Freeze-numbness, inability to move or speak.

A fourth response, described separately, is fawning: appeasing the person who poses the threat in order to reduce the danger. It often develops in childhood, when there is nowhere to run.

None of these is a conscious choice, and that is worth telling the client directly. Shame is most often tied to freezing: “why didn’t I do anything, why did I stay silent.” Freezing is neither consent nor weakness, but an automatic response chosen not by the person but by their nervous system.

In therapy: the client learns to recognize their typical response, notice its early signs in the body and complete unfinished mobilization-through movement, sound, action.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The fight-or-flight response was described by Walter Cannon (1915); freezing and its role in trauma were examined in detail by Peter Levine; the term “fawn” was proposed by Pete Walker.
