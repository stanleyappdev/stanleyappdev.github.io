Working with guilt and shame

  • Responsibility
  • Self-acceptance
  • Self-compassion
  • Shame and guilt

Guilt is about an action (“I did something bad”), shame is about the whole self (“I am bad”). Guilt can often be balanced: by apologising, putting things right, making amends. Shame is felt as a threat to the very right to be accepted, so it eases mainly through contact: when a person is seen and others do not turn away.

In session: separate real responsibility for the action from an internalised verdict on oneself, and find out whose voice is speaking in that verdict.

How to explain it to a client

“Guilt says: I did something wrong, and it can be put right. Shame says: something is wrong with me. Let’s see which part concerns the action and which part is a judgement of you as a person.”

An example

“I shouted at my son” is guilt, which may lead to an apology and a different way of acting next time. “I’m a terrible mother” is shame, which suggests nothing and only wears you out.

Questions

  • ✓What exactly did you do or not do?
  • ✓What of it can be put right, and how?
  • ✓Whose voice speaks when you condemn yourself?
  • ✓What would you say to someone close to you who had done the same?

Note for the practitioner

Shame is often hard to speak about aloud. Slow down, do not ask for details, and notice when the client avoids your eyes or withdraws.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The distinction between shame and guilt is described in June Tangney’s research and in Brené Brown’s work; in Gestalt, shame is viewed as a contact-boundary phenomenon.
