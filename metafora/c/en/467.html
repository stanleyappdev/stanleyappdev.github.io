Trauma and its impact on the mind

  • PTSD
  • Recovery
  • Safety

Trauma is not the event itself but the fact that the psyche couldn’t cope with it: the experience remains unprocessed and continues to affect the present.

Signs: intrusive memories and flashbacks, avoidance of everything that serves as a reminder, constant vigilance, emotional constriction, changes in how one sees oneself and the world.

It is important to distinguish a single-incident trauma from a prolonged one that arises in relationships: domestic violence, chronic devaluation, childhood neglect. The second often has a deeper effect, because the source of threat coincides with the source of safety.

Not every hard event leads to a disorder: most people recover on their own, especially when they have support.

The phase-oriented approach structures the work this way: first stabilization and resources, only then processing of memories, and finally integration and a return to life. Trying to begin with processing without stabilization can lead to retraumatization. Some evidence-based methods move to memories earlier, but the client’s safety and stability still come first.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The phase-oriented approach to trauma goes back to Pierre Janet’s work in the late 19th century; the modern formulation of the three phases was given by Judith Herman in Trauma and Recovery (1992). Signs of PTSD are summarised from ICD-11 and DSM-5.
