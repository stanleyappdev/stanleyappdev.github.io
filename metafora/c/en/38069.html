A child after a frightening event: a guide for parents

  • PTSD
  • Upbringing
  • Support
  • Children's fears

A handout for parents whose child has gone through or witnessed a frightening event: an accident, a fire, a dog attack, violence at home.

What can happen

- Preschoolers cling to an adult, act younger than their age, replay the event in play again and again, are afraid to sleep alone.

- Younger school-age children ask the same questions several times, complain of stomachaches or headaches, get irritable, blame themselves.

- Teens withdraw, take risks, get angry, say “I don’t care.”

What helps

- A predictable routine and familiar rules: they are calming.

- Brief, honest, age-appropriate information and patient answers to repeated questions.

- Don’t forbid play about the event. If the play gets “stuck” and the child becomes frightened in it, gently add a rescuer or a different ending.

- Praise the child when they cope: “You were scared, but you told me, and that was brave.”

- Shield the child from adult conversations, news and videos about the event.

- Don’t make them talk about it, but stay close.

- Take care of yourself: your calm passes on to the child.

When something reminds the child of the event (a smell, a place), name it: “That reminded you of what happened. You’re safe now, I’m here.”

You can seek professional help right away. Be sure to do so if the child is getting worse or is no better after a month, has stopped playing or going to preschool or school, is constantly afraid, or keeps reliving the event. If anything threatens the child’s safety, seek help immediately.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

Draws on the psychoeducation and parenting skills component of TF-CBT; Judith Cohen, Anthony Mannarino, Esther Deblinger, Treating Trauma and Traumatic Grief in Children and Adolescents (2006).
