Window of tolerance

  • Safety
  • Panic attacks
  • Self-regulation
  • Self-soothing
  • Body awareness
  • PTSD

The window of tolerance is the range of arousal within which a person can think, feel and act at the same time. Inside the window, both emotions and reason are available.

Above the window is hyperarousal: panic, rage, insomnia, inability to sit still. Below it is hypoarousal: numbness, emptiness, a sense that “I’m not here,” heaviness in the body. In both states, therapeutic conversation is of little use: the person first needs to get back into the window.

In people with traumatic experience the window is often narrower, and they leave it more quickly. Widening the window is one of the goals of therapy, and it happens gradually.

How to get back: from above, bring it down-a longer exhale, slow movement, grounding into a supporting surface; from below, bring it up-the opposite, activation: cold water, brisk movement, a strong taste, eye contact.

It helps the client to learn to notice their own signs of leaving the window before the state becomes unmanageable.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The concept of the window of tolerance was introduced by Dan Siegel; in trauma work it was developed by Pat Ogden and Janina Fisher.
