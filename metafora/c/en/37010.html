Exercise

STOP

  • Self-soothing
  • Tolerating difficult emotions
  • Anger
  • Control

Goal: learn a short sequence of actions for the few seconds between a strong impulse and acting on it, so as not to do something at the peak of emotion that you later regret.

Approximate time: 20-30 minutes in session, then practice in everyday life.

Materials: paper and a pen; optionally a sticker or reminder card.

The few seconds between an impulse and an action often decide how an evening ends: with a sharp reply, a slammed door, a message you later regret. STOP is a crisis survival skill for exactly those seconds, simple enough to remember at the peak of emotion.

S (Stop): stop. Literally freeze: don’t speak, don’t move, don’t press “send”.

T (Take a step back): step back. Take a step back, leave the room, put the phone down, breathe out slowly. The aim is to buy time.

O (Observe): observe. What is happening inside you (thoughts, sensations, urges) and around you? What exactly did the other person say or do?

P (Proceed mindfully): proceed mindfully. What do I want to achieve? What will make the situation better, and what will make it worse?

  1. 1.Go through a recent episode

Take an episode in which the impulse won and “rewind” it: where could the client have frozen, where could they have stepped back to, what could they have noticed, which action could they have chosen? Write it down under the four letters.

  1. 2.Rehearse in motion

The psychologist or a group member says an irritating phrase, agreed in advance and without insults. The client freezes, takes a step back and breathes out. Repeat two or three times, then add a short spoken description of what the client noticed and the action they chose.

  1. 3.Discuss
  • ✓Which letter was hardest?
  • ✓What did you notice in your body when you froze?
  • ✓What signs tell you that the impulse is close?
  • ✓In which situations this week might you need STOP the most?
  1. 4.Close

Agree on a reminder: a word, a gesture, a sticker on the phone case. The client names one situation in which they will try the skill and at the next meeting tells you what worked.

With teenagers

The image of pausing a computer game helps: you press pause, and there is time to think before your character makes a move. Practise with situations from their lives: an argument with parents, a hurtful comment in a chat, a quarrel with a friend.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The STOP skill from the distress tolerance module; Marsha Linehan, DBT Skills Training Manual (2015).
