Fairy tale

Thumbelina

  • Right to choose
  • Personal boundaries
  • Your own wants
  • Support

A woman who longed for a child planted a barleycorn she had been given by a witch. A flower grew from it, and in the flower sat a tiny girl no bigger than a thumb. But one night an old toad stole her to marry her to its son. The girl was put on a water-lily leaf in the middle of the river, and the fish, feeling sorry for her, gnawed through the stem.

From then on, others kept deciding her fate. A cockchafer carried her up a tree but dropped her because his relatives said she was ugly. In winter a field mouse took her in and set about marrying Thumbelina to a rich blind mole who despised the sun and flowers. In a dark underground passage the girl found a frozen swallow and secretly nursed it all winter.

In spring the swallow invited her to fly away with it, but Thumbelina did not want to upset the mouse and stayed. Only on her wedding day, as she was saying goodbye to the sun, did she hear the swallow again, and this time she flew. In a warm land among the flowers she met the tiny king of the flower elves, and there she was given wings of her own.

Questions

  • ✓Who in the story made decisions for Thumbelina, and how did she feel about it?
  • ✓Why didn’t she fly away with the swallow the first time?
  • ✓What changed when she finally chose for herself?
  • ✓Whom did Thumbelina help, and how did it come back to her later?

The story helps you talk with children and teenagers about other people’s plans for their lives, about gratitude that turns into dependence, and about the right to choose.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

Hans Christian Andersen, “Thumbelina” (Tommelise, 1835). Free retelling by Metafora.
