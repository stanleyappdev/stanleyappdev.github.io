Exercise

Double standard technique

  • Perfectionism
  • Self-criticism
  • Responsibility
  • Self-compassion

Many people demand of themselves what they would never demand of others: they believe they must answer calls immediately, yet they don’t get angry when others don’t answer theirs. The exercise makes this difference visible and helps the client choose one standard for themselves and others.

Part 1. Rules

Divide a sheet into two columns.

  1. 1.On the left, write down 3–5 rules for yourself with the words “I have to,” “I must,” “I mustn’t”: “I have to reply to messages within an hour,” “I mustn’t show up to a meeting unprepared.”
  1. 2.On the right, opposite each one: how do you react when a friend, colleague or partner doesn’t follow that rule?

Part 2. Conversation

  1. 3.Recall a recent mistake and write down, word for word, what you said to yourself at the time.
  1. 4.Imagine the same thing happened to a close friend. What would you say to them? Compare the words, the tone and the conclusions.

Part 3. One standard

  1. 5.For each rule, write a version that is fair both to you and to others: “I answer when I can and call back within the day - just as I expect of others.”

It also happens the other way around: a person is demanding of others but lenient with themselves; then the shared standard is found in the same way. In a group, it helps to read a few rules out loud: someone else’s “must” usually sounds too harsh the moment you hear it.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The double standard technique; David D. Burns, The Feeling Good Handbook (1989).
