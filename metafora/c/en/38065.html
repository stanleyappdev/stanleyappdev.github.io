Exercise

Archive of personal victories

  • Self-confidence
  • Resources
  • Inner support
  • Self-criticism

The archive keeps stories rather than a list of achievements: each entry is a small document about what the person can already do, as opposed to what the problem says about them.

How to run

  1. 1.Choose where to keep the archive: a notebook, a folder, a note on their phone. On the first page, write down in the problem’s own words what it says about the client: “You’ll never manage,” “You always give up.”
  1. 2.Make the first entry in session: describe a situation when things were hard, yet the person coped or didn’t give up. Small stories work too: returned a faulty item, was the first to apologize, arranged a retake with a teacher on their own.
  1. 3.Every entry has the same lines:

- title of the story;

- what was hard;

- what I did, step by step;

- what helped;

- what skill this shows: “I can ask for help before everything falls apart”;

- who witnessed it; if they wish, this person adds two or three lines of their own.

  1. 4.Between sessions, the client adds new and old stories. This is also the place for the client’s apt phrases that you noted down in session, and for messages from others.
  1. 5.Every few weeks, reread the archive and put together a page called “What I can already do”: write the recurring skills as advice to someone facing the same problem.
  1. 6.Agree on when to open the archive: before a difficult task or when the voice of the problem gets louder again.

Questions

  • ✓Which story in the archive most contradicts what the problem says about you?

What to watch for

“Victories” here are the person’s own steps, not victories over others. A teen’s archive is private; parents don’t check it.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

Draws on the idea of archives and therapeutic documents in David Epston’s narrative practice.
