Self-compassion as a skill

  • Self-criticism
  • Self-acceptance
  • Self-compassion
  • Supportive self-talk
  • Shame and guilt
  • Perfectionism

Self-compassion has three components: self-kindness instead of self-judgment, recognition of common humanity instead of the feeling that “this only happens to me,” and mindfulness instead of over-identification with the experience.

The most common resistance: “if I stop beating myself up, I’ll slack off and achieve nothing.” Research shows the opposite: self-compassion is linked to greater persistence after failure, because it doesn’t require defending against shame.

Self-compassion is not the same as self-pity and doesn’t cancel responsibility. It changes the tone in which a person talks to themselves about a mistake, not the fact of the mistake.

A simple exercise to start with: recall what you would say to a close friend in this same situation, and say it to yourself out loud in the very same words.

An important warning: in people with early traumatic experiences, first attempts at kindness toward themselves can bring up tears or anxiety. This is a known reaction, not a sign that something is wrong.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

Kristin Neff’s model of self-compassion; the backlash reaction to compassion (backdraft) was described by Christopher Germer, and fears of compassion by Paul Gilbert.
