Exercise

Who is Grumbles?

  • Aggression in children
  • Anger
  • Self-regulation
  • Responsibility

A child who is constantly scolded for outbursts starts to believe they are “bad.” Externalizing separates the child from the problem: anger becomes a character that the child and the adults stand up to together.

How to run

  1. 1.Ask: “Does it ever happen that anger gets so big it’s as if someone grabs the steering wheel and makes you shout? What could we call him?” The child comes up with the name: Grumbles, Growler, Prickles.
  1. 2.Ask the child to draw or sculpt him, and ask questions like an explorer: “When does he come - when you’re hungry, tired, when your brother takes your things? What does he whisper to you?”
  1. 3.The most important part is the exceptions: “Has it ever happened that he came, but you outsmarted him?” Ask in detail how the child managed it and who was nearby.
  1. 4.Make a “plan against Grumbles”: two or three ways that have already worked, and which adults are on the team.
  1. 5.At later sessions ask: “Who won more often this week - you or Grumbles?”

With preschoolers (from 4–5 years old), keep the talk shorter and work more through drawing and toys. The child is still responsible: if Grumbles won, the child and the adults think together about how to put things right. Advise parents to say “Looks like Grumbles has come” instead of “You’re behaving terribly again.”

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

Externalizing conversations are a narrative therapy method developed by Michael White and David Epston, described in Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends (1990).
