Exercise

Downward arrow

  • Awareness of thoughts
  • Self-worth
  • Anxious thoughts
  • Self-criticism

The technique leads from an automatic thought to the core belief that gives it its power.

How to run

  1. 1.At the top of a sheet, write down an automatic thought that triggered a strong emotion.
  1. 2.Ask: “Suppose this is true. What does it mean to you? Why does it upset you so much?” Write the answer below, under an arrow pointing down.
  1. 3.Ask the same question of each new answer.

Example (a friend has canceled plans for the second time)

“She doesn’t want to see me anymore.”

↓ “I’ve disappointed her somehow.”

↓ “I always disappoint the people I care about.”

↓ “I’m unlovable: sooner or later everyone leaves.”

When to stop

- the answer sounds like a general rule about yourself, people or the world: “I’m worthless,” “People can’t be trusted”;

- the answers have started to repeat;

- the emotion has noticeably intensified, and the client says, “That’s it.”

What next

Don’t challenge the belief right away: write it down and ask the client to rate how strongly they believe it, from 0 to 100%. Then work with it gradually: evidence, a balanced reformulation, behavioral experiments.

Caution: the technique quickly brings painful beliefs to the surface. Use it when the therapeutic relationship is stable, ask permission at every step, and leave time at the end of the session for the client to settle.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The downward arrow (vertical arrow) technique as described by David D. Burns in Feeling Good: The New Mood Therapy (1980).
