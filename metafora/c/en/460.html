What emotions are and why we need them

  • Children's emotions
  • Fear
  • Anger
  • Sadness
  • Joy
  • Tolerating difficult emotions
  • Recognizing and naming emotions

An emotion is a quick appraisal of a situation that prepares the body for action. It often arises faster than we can think anything through, which is why it feels as if it “sweeps over” us.

Each basic emotion has a function. Fear prepares us to avoid a threat. Anger mobilizes us to defend a boundary. Sadness slows us down and calls for support after a loss. Disgust turns us away from what is harmful. Shame keeps us within the group. Joy reinforces what is worth repeating.

There are no good or bad emotions. The problem arises when an emotion doesn’t fit the situation in intensity or content: fear without a threat, shame without a violation of one’s own values, anger at someone who isn’t to blame.

What to do with it: first name it (“this is anger”), then check the facts (“about what exactly”), and only then decide whether to act on the impulse. Research on affect labelling shows that putting a feeling into words can slightly reduce its intensity.

A common request from clients is “teach me not to feel”. Emotions cannot be switched off selectively: constant attempts to suppress unpleasant feelings often dull pleasant ones too.

With a child aged 7-10

Explain briefly: “Feelings are like signals. Fear tells you to be careful, anger that something doesn’t suit you, sadness that you need support.” Ask the child to show where in the body they feel each feeling, and name it together.

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The functional view of emotions draws on Paul Ekman’s work on basic emotions and Matthew Lieberman’s research on naming emotions (affect labeling).
