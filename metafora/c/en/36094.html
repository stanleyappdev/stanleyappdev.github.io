Conflict or bullying

  • Bullying
  • Conflict

An explanation for students, parents, and teachers. Arguments and bullying are often confused: real bullying gets written off as “the kids just had a fight,” while an ordinary argument gets blown up into bullying.

Signs of bullying

- it is repeated, not a one-time event;

- there is a power imbalance: the one doing the bullying is older, stronger, more popular, or not acting alone;

- the goal is to humiliate, frighten, or exclude;

- bystanders’ laughter or silence supports the bully.

Conflict is a clash between roughly equal sides: both stand up for their position, and both can give way and come to an agreement. A joke differs from mockery in that both people find it funny.

Bullying can be physical, verbal, social (exclusion, gossip), or online.

Why it matters: a conflict is resolved by the two sides talking it out; bullying is not. A “make up with each other” meeting puts the child face to face with the stronger party and often makes their situation worse. Stopping bullying is the adults’ job, and the school is obliged to respond.

Questions for discussion

- Recall a story from a film or book: is it conflict or bullying? What are the signs?

SOURCE AND ORIGIN

The definition of bullying by repetition, intent, and power imbalance is based on Dan Olweus.
